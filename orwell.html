The Man Behind The Name

Eric Blair Wrote as George Orwell. We Kept the Name.

Orwell wrote two dystopian novels. He also wrote five other novels, several book-length works of reportage, and hundreds of essays — most of which never mention a telescreen. This page is the record of all of it, not just the two books everyone quotes.

Animal Farm

Nineteen Eighty-Four
Written as extrapolation, not prophecy — Orwell took the propaganda and surveillance methods he'd already watched in wartime Britain and the Soviet Union and asked what a government perfecting them would look like.

NOT DYSTOPIAN — NOT IN THE CANON
1934
Burmese Days
Drawn from Orwell's five years as an imperial policeman in Burma — a novel about colonial rule that reads less like fiction and more like an eyewitness account of how power corrodes the people who administer it.
Novel
1935
A Clergyman's Daughter
His most experimental and least-loved novel, including a stretch written entirely as unattributed dialogue. Orwell later tried to have it kept out of print.
Novel
1936
Keep the Aspidistra Flying
A satire of literary ambition and genteel poverty — a bookseller's assistant declares war on money, then discovers what actually happens to people who opt out of earning it.
Novel
1939
Coming Up for Air
A middle-aged insurance salesman returns to his childhood town looking for the England he remembers, on the eve of a war Orwell could already see coming.

NONFICTION

Where the Politics Came From

REPORTAGE AND MEMOIR
1933
Down and Out in Paris and London
A memoir of deliberate poverty — Orwell worked as a dishwasher in Paris and tramped London's streets, and wrote down exactly how the system treated people who had nothing.
Memoir
1937
The Road to Wigan Pier
Commissioned as a report on unemployment in industrial northern England; the second half turned into an argument with the socialist movement Orwell was nominally writing for, about who it was actually built to serve.
Reportage
1938
Homage to Catalonia
His account of fighting in the Spanish Civil War, including watching allied factions turn on each other — the direct source of his lifelong distrust of political language used to disguise what's actually happening.
Memoir
ESSAYS

The Shorter Arguments

WHERE THE VOCABULARY CAME FROM
1936
Shooting an Elephant
Orwell, as a colonial officer, shoots an elephant he doesn't need to kill because a crowd expects him to — an essay about how authority traps the people who hold it as much as the people under it.
Essay
1941
England Your England
Written during the Blitz, an attempt to define a national character worth defending without pretending the country had no serious problems of its own.
Essay
1945
Notes on Nationalism
Distinguishes nationalism from patriotism — the first defined by a need to see one's own side as always right, the second by love of a place that doesn't require thinking it's superior to anywhere else.
Essay
1946
Politics and the English Language
The essay this whole site's vocabulary comes from — the argument that vague, euphemistic language isn't just bad writing, it's how political actors make cruelty and absurdity sound reasonable.
Essay
1946
Why I Write
Orwell's own account of what pulled him toward writing, and his claim that after 1936 every serious line he wrote was written, directly or indirectly, against totalitarianism.
Essay
